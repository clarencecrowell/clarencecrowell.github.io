Past the GrassA Triptych

Three linked parts: The Tracks Ended Before Noon, All Laid Out for You, and The Shape of Trees. A name worn down across generations until it isn’t one anymore.

Complete
Excerpt
From Book One, The Tracks Ended Before Noon, Chapter 19

The basin ended without warning. The land rose again in broken shelves of stone and gravel. The horse stumbled once and he dismounted again and walked beside it.

He found the bones of a mule beside the trail. The harness was still attached but the leather had hardened and cracked.

He stood looking at it longer than he meant to.

He cut the straps loose and set them aside.

He rode on.

He began to see shapes in the distance that did not change as he approached them.

Once he followed a dark line for most of the afternoon before discovering it was only a seam in the ground where the soil had split.

Another time he rode toward what he thought was water until the light shifted and it disappeared.

He stopped looking toward the horizon after that.

The horse drank the last of the water sooner than he expected.

He waited before opening the canteen again.

When he did the water was warm.

He held it in his mouth before swallowing.

He thought about the ditch. Water moving beside him. Cottonwoods along the bank.

He fell asleep thinking of it.

When he woke there was no sound at all.

He found tracks crossing a basin.

They were fresh enough that the edges had not yet fallen in.

He followed them until they split in two directions and then he stopped.

He chose one without thinking about it.

The tracks ended before noon.

He turned once in the saddle and could not tell which way he had come.

The wind rose that afternoon and did not stop.

It carried dust low along the ground and filled the horse's nostrils. It settled along the seams of his coat and in the folds of the saddle blanket.

He rode with his head down.

Once he thought he saw someone standing along the edge of the basin but when he reached the place there was nothing there.

He did not turn to look behind him.

He crossed a dry lake that held the marks of old wagon wheels pressed deep into the surface. The lines ran straight toward the far side and then stopped together as though the wagons had been lifted into the air.

He followed them until they disappeared.

He stood there for a long time before riding on.

The horse began to lag.

He loosened the cinch and walked beside it again.

The ground rose slowly ahead of them.

He did not see when the basin ended.

Only that the air felt different once they had left it behind.

There was grass again.

02

InholdingThe Miserable Acres

Far west Texas, 1994. Two men whose fathers killed each other over land that became a national park. A wildfire, a ranch, and a reckoning neither one expects.

Draft 2 — In Revision
Excerpt
From the fire

The cattle were already moving when they found them. Alexander could hear them. The bawling carried through the dark country. Not the occasional cry of a calf looking for its mother. Constant. Nervous. Hundreds of animals speaking over one another.

The smoke was thick enough now that the stars disappeared whenever the wind shifted. The fire was somewhere beyond the ridges to the west. They could not see the flames from here but they could see the sky. Orange and pulsing. Every few moments the glow brightened and the shadows beneath the horses shifted. Chuck watched it for a long time. Then he spat.

“Shit.”

The paint was uneasy beneath Alexander. She kept wanting to turn east. The quarter horse was the same. Neither horse liked the smell.

The radio crackled. Just fragments and words buried beneath static.

Everything between them and the fire was dry enough to burn. A gust pushed smoke through the draw. The herd immediately bunched tighter. A calf broke into a run. Then half a dozen. The entire herd began to shift.

The men kicked their horses forward. Alexander rode hard along the southern edge. The paint stretched beneath him. Dust exploded from beneath her hooves. The cattle pushed together so tightly their bodies looked like moving water. A steer crashed through a patch of greasewood. A cow jumped a wash she normally would have crossed slowly. The sky flashed.

The ridge behind them glowed bright orange. For a moment Alexander could see individual trees burning. Then the smoke swallowed them again. The sound arrived several seconds later. Not a roar.

Alexander looked back once.

“Jesus Christ.”

The fire had crossed the ridge. Alexander felt something cold settle in his stomach. The flames were moving fast. Entire hillsides glowed. Trees appeared as black silhouettes for a moment before disappearing into sparks. Ash began falling. The cattle lost all composure.

The ground shook beneath hundreds of hooves. Dust and ash mixed together. The air became difficult to breathe. Alexander rode alongside a terrified cow and calf. The calf stumbled. The mother refused to leave it. She kept nudging it forward.

The fence appeared suddenly through the smoke. Chuck was already there. He was cutting the wires. The wire recoiled into darkness. The cattle poured through the opening. Bodies packed shoulder to shoulder. Some hit the posts hard enough to shake them. One snapped entirely.

Alexander looked back. The fire had reached the ridge where they had started. He could see individual junipers exploding into flame. Thousands of sparks lifted into the night. The entire eastern horizon burned. For a moment it looked impossible.

Chuck climbed back into the saddle. Neither man spoke. They turned their horses after the cattle and rode west beneath a sky that glowed orange.

Butch

A Pinkerton detective in Gloucester, Massachusetts, 1922. Pulp, not polish.

In Progress
Excerpt
Prologue

There was nothing in the boat but old blood and scraps of entrails, some bits were floating in dirty bilge water. The man had hauled every line and come up empty. Billy Putter was sipping from a flask and headed to the harbor. His body was hunched forward, showing the toll fishing takes on a body as it hits middle age. Above him the sky was huge and empty. Billy pulled the old hat down lower on his head and tightened his jacket. The early fall had always bothered Billy. It wasn’t cold exactly but something in the wind shot right into his bones.

He was nursing the flask tenderly; the small tin container had been a gift from his wife after his first day on the sea in his own boat. He thought of home, there was a full bottle waiting for him and hopefully something to eat. The whiskey was warm and he felt that heat in his stomach, it was keeping him going.

The wind was blowing toward home but the breeze was light and it was getting very late. It was already dark. A small lamp made little bulges in the sea dance. The sea was quiet except for the sound of the small sail catching the air and the waves lapping alongside the boat. Billy began humming a tune and scanning the horizon behind him. There was a thick fog overtaking him. He squinted and watched the soupy grey blob gather and build itself larger, it rose up and didn’t quite blow in on him. It darkened the night even farther. He shuddered and tightened the sail.

Ahead of him the harbor was glowing faintly. The glow was warm and milky. Billy wanted to tie the boat and start in on the bottle at home. He scanned the empty buckets in front of him and sighed. He knew he could tie the boat, pull the sail and simply start walking home. He shook the flask, about half left, he sipped. The boat stopped and Billy tumbled forward. The flask fell into the water and slowly sank into the dark water.

Billy rose and sat on his knees. He touched a large gash on his forehead. His vision was blurry and blood was pouring into his right eye. He tried to pat around for the flask but he could not find it. The sail was still catching the wind but the boat was refusing to move. The little mast was creaking under the pressure. Billy looked into the blackness below the port side, his eye was full of blood. He scooped a handful of water and tried to wash the cut. There was a glint in the water below him, it was his flask. He realized he may have run aground, perhaps a sandbar had shifted or he lost his way in the fog. He reached into the water and pulled the flask up from under the surface. The whiskey inside sloshed against the metal. Billy stared at it. Looking past it he could see something pale was moving beneath the boat.
